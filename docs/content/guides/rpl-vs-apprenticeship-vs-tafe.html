RPL, an apprenticeship and a TAFE course can all lead to the same nationally recognised qualification. The difference is how you get there. RPL assesses skills you already have, an apprenticeship teaches you on the job while you earn, and TAFE or another training provider teaches you through structured classes. The right choice depends mostly on one thing: how much experience you already have.

The quick answer

  • Already experienced in the work? RPL is usually the fastest route, because you are assessed on what you already know rather than taught it again.
  • New to a trade and want to earn while you learn? An apprenticeship is built for you.
  • New to an industry and want structured learning? A TAFE or other provider course gives you teaching, classes and support.
  • Somewhere in between? Combine them: get RPL for the units you can already do, and train for the rest.

RPL: for skills you already have

RPL suits people who already do the work. Instead of training, you show evidence of your skills, and a qualified assessor at a registered training organisation checks it against each unit of the qualification. If your experience covers all of them, you get the full qualification without attending a class. Read what is RPL? for the full explanation.

Pros: fast, no classes, you keep working, and you don't pay to learn what you already know.

Cons: it needs genuine experience and good evidence. If you are new to the work, RPL will leave gaps.

Apprenticeship: learn a trade while you earn

An apprenticeship combines paid work with structured training under a training contract between you, your employer and a training provider. It is the traditional route into trades such as carpentry, plumbing, electrical work, automotive and commercial cookery.

Pros: you earn a wage, learn from experienced tradespeople, and finish with the skills, experience and qualification licensing bodies expect. Government incentives may support apprentices and employers.

Cons: it takes years, you need an employer willing to take you on, and apprentice wages are lower than a qualified tradesperson's. For someone who already has years of experience, repeating the whole apprenticeship is usually unnecessary.

TAFE and other training providers: structured learning

TAFE institutes and private registered training organisations teach qualifications through classes, online learning and work placements. Some courses are subsidised, and fee-free places are available in certain courses for eligible students.

Pros: structured teaching, support from trainers, and a good foundation if you are starting from scratch or changing careers.

Cons: it takes longer, has set timetables, and can be hard to fit around full-time work and family.

Many TAFEs and private providers also offer RPL. RPL is a way of being assessed, not a type of institution.

Combining the pathways

The pathways aren't all-or-nothing. Many people use a mix:

  • RPL plus gap training. You get RPL for most units and train only for the few you haven't done.
  • Credit transfer. Units you have already completed in another qualification count towards the new one, so you don't redo them.
  • Apprentices who are partway through. If you have done a large part of an apprenticeship but didn't finish, RPL may recognise what you have already learned on the job.

Do employers value RPL as much as an apprenticeship?

The qualification is the same, so on paper there is no difference. What employers care about is whether you can do the job, and RPL candidates have usually proven that already through years of work. For licensed trades, regulators may also look at your practical experience, which is why keeping good records of your work matters whichever pathway you choose.

Making the decision

Ask yourself three questions:

  1. Have I already been doing this work for a few years?
  2. Can I get evidence and references to prove it?
  3. Do I need the qualification soon, for a job, promotion, licence or visa?
