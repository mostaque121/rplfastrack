To get RPL in Australia, you choose the qualification that matches your experience, apply to a registered training organisation (RTO), collect evidence of your skills, and complete an assessment. If the assessor finds you competent in every unit, the RTO issues your nationally recognised qualification. The whole process happens without classes, and most of it can be done online.

This guide walks through each step, what to expect, and the mistakes that slow people down.

Step 1: Check you are eligible

You are eligible for RPL if your experience covers what a qualification's units of competency describe. There is no fixed number of years that applies to every qualification. The real test is whether you have done the tasks in each unit, recently enough and often enough to do them well.

Before you apply, ask yourself:

  • How long have I been doing this kind of work, and in what roles?
  • Which tasks do I do every week, and which have I only done occasionally?
  • Who could vouch for my work: a supervisor, employer, client or colleague?
  • What records do I already have: photos, job sheets, reports, tickets, certificates?

A free eligibility check takes these answers and maps them to a qualification. It is the quickest way to find out whether RPL is realistic for you before you spend time on evidence.

Step 2: Choose the right qualification

The right qualification is the one that matches the work you actually do, at the level you do it. Picking the wrong one is the most common reason RPL takes longer than it should.

  • Certificate III usually matches a qualified tradesperson or skilled worker, for example the Certificate III in Carpentry or the Certificate III in Individual Support.
  • Certificate IV usually matches someone who supervises, plans or takes responsibility for others' work, such as the Cert IV in Building and Construction, the Cert IV in Ageing Support or the Certificate IV in Business.
  • Diploma and above usually match managers and senior specialists who make decisions, manage budgets or lead teams.

Also check the qualification code. Training packages are updated, and older codes are replaced by newer ones. Employers and regulators generally want the current version. Each course page on this site shows the current national code.

If your job needs a licence (electrical, plumbing, building, security and others), confirm which qualification your state's regulator asks for before you start.

Step 3: Apply and get your USI

To be issued any nationally recognised qualification, you need a Unique Student Identifier (USI). It is free, takes a few minutes to create on the government's USI website, and stays with you for life. If you have done nationally recognised training since 2015, you probably already have one.

When you apply, the RTO will also ask for proof of identity, your contact details and some background information. They may check your language, literacy and numeracy so the assessment suits you. None of this is a test you can fail; it helps the RTO support you properly.

Step 4: Collect your evidence

Evidence is the heart of RPL, and it is where most of your time goes. You will usually receive an evidence guide or portfolio template that lists what the assessor needs for each unit.

Step 5: Complete the assessment

The assessment is where a qualified assessor confirms that your evidence reflects your real skills. It usually has up to three parts:

  1. Portfolio review. The assessor works through your evidence unit by unit and notes anything missing.
  2. Competency conversation. A structured interview, often by video call, where you explain how you do the job, how you handle problems and how you follow safety and quality rules. It is a conversation about your work, not a quiz.
  3. Practical demonstration or workplace observation. For hands-on qualifications, you may be asked to show a task in person or on video, or a supervisor may complete a third-party report.

Many people find the competency conversation the easiest part, because they are talking about work they do every day. Answer with real examples: what you did, why, and what the result was.

Step 6: Close any gaps

If the assessor finds gaps, you are told exactly which units need more evidence or training. You can usually close them by supplying extra evidence, such as another reference or more photos, or by doing short gap training for those units only. You never have to repeat units you have already been granted.

Gaps are normal. They are most common in units you rarely do in your current job, such as quoting, compliance paperwork or a specialist task.

Step 7: Receive your qualification

When every unit is complete, the RTO issues your testamur (certificate) and record of results. These are the same documents a student who studied the course would receive, and the qualification is recorded against your USI.

If you need the qualification for a licence, the next step is your state or territory regulator's application. Read our guides on the electrical licence through RPL and the plumbing licence through RPL for what that usually involves.

How long does each step take?

RPL usually takes weeks, not months. The eligibility check is quick, and the assessment itself is often a few sessions. The variable part is evidence collection, which depends on how organised your records are and how quickly your referees reply.

  • Start collecting evidence as soon as you decide to go ahead.
  • Give referees a short template so they know exactly what to write.
  • Book your competency conversation early and keep the appointment.

Mistakes that slow RPL down

  • Choosing a qualification above or below your experience. Too high and you end up with gaps; too low and you waste a good portfolio.
  • Sending payslips instead of evidence of skill. Payslips prove employment, not competence.
  • Vague references. "John is a hard worker" doesn't help. A good reference describes the tasks you did, for how long, and to what standard.
  • Old evidence only. If most of your evidence is from many years ago, add something recent.
  • Unlabelled uploads. Name each file by what it shows and which unit it supports.
