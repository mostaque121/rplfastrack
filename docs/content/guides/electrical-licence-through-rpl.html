You can use RPL to gain the qualification an electrical licence needs, the UEE30820 Certificate III in Electrotechnology Electrician, but the licence itself comes from your state or territory regulator, not from the training organisation. Electrical work is one of the most tightly regulated trades in Australia, so RPL here is stricter than in most industries. This guide explains how the pathway works, who it realistically suits, and what each state's regulator usually expects.

Electrical work must only be done by a licensed electrician, or by an apprentice or trainee under proper supervision. RPL recognises legitimate experience; it is never a way around those rules.

Qualification vs licence: two different things

The qualification proves you are competent; the licence gives you the legal right to do electrical work. They are issued by different bodies:

  • The qualification (UEE30820 Certificate III in Electrotechnology Electrician) is issued by a registered training organisation (RTO) after you meet every unit, whether by training, RPL or a mix.
  • The licence is issued by the electrical regulator in your state or territory once you meet its requirements, which include the qualification and usually more.

RPL can shorten the first part a lot for people with the right experience. It doesn't replace the second.

Who can realistically get UEE30820 through RPL?

RPL for the electrician qualification suits people who have already done a wide range of electrical work under appropriate supervision, typically:

  • apprentices who completed most of their training and work but never finished the qualification;
  • workers who did electrical work as apprentices or trainees but changed employer or state before completing;
  • electricians who qualified overseas and need the Australian qualification, often alongside a skills assessment;
  • people who hold an older electrical qualification and need the current one for their regulator.

It usually does not suit someone with general handyman or "electrical-adjacent" experience, because the units cover wiring, testing, fault-finding, switchboards, safety and compliance in depth, and the evidence has to show supervised, compliant work.

What the electrical RPL assessment involves

Expect more practical assessment than in most RPL. A typical assessment combines:

  1. A detailed work history, often with a logbook or work profile that shows the range of electrical tasks you have done and who supervised you.
  2. Third-party reports from licensed electricians or electrical contractors who supervised your work.
  3. Photos, job records and test results that show your installations, testing and documentation.
  4. A competency conversation on wiring rules, safe isolation, testing sequences and fault-finding.
  5. Practical and capstone assessment. UEE30820 includes a final capstone assessment of safe, compliant work and testing, which you complete under observation. In Victoria, Energy Safe Victoria also runs its own Licensed Electrician's Assessment (LEA).

Because of the practical components, electrical RPL is rarely 100% online. Your evidence and interview can usually be done remotely, but the practical and capstone parts generally need you in person.

Electrical licensing regulators, state by state

Each state and territory licenses electricians separately. The usual requirements are the current electrician qualification, evidence of practical experience and the regulator's own application. Overseas electricians

If you qualified as an electrician overseas, you will usually need both an Australian skills assessment and an Australian licence. Trades Recognition Australia runs assessment pathways for overseas electricians, and many states grant a provisional or restricted licence first so you can work under supervision while you complete the Australian requirements, such as gap training and the capstone assessment.

RPL can recognise much of your overseas experience towards UEE30820, especially if you have detailed records, references and photos of your work. Our guide on how to get RPL in Australia explains the general process, and the evidence guide shows how to present work from overseas.

Restricted licences and related trades

Not every electrical task needs a full electrician's licence. Some trades work under a restricted electrical licence, which covers a narrow range of work related to their trade, such as disconnecting and reconnecting equipment.

A common example is air conditioning and refrigeration. Technicians often hold the UEE32225 Certificate III in Air Conditioning and Refrigeration, a refrigerant handling licence from the Australian Refrigeration Council, and, in many states, a restricted electrical licence. If that describes your work, that qualification may be a better fit than the full electrician pathway.

How to prepare for electrical RPL

  • Get your supervisors on board early. Reports from licensed electricians who oversaw your work are the most important evidence you have.
  • Collect test records and job sheets. Insulation resistance, earth continuity and polarity results show that you test as well as install.
  • Photograph your work properly. Before, during and after shots of switchboards, circuits and installations, with client details hidden.
  • Revise the wiring rules. Your competency conversation and capstone will test current standards, not how things were done years ago.
  • Check your regulator first. Confirm exactly what it requires so you're not surprised after you have the qualification.

Is the RPL pathway right for you?

If you have substantial, supervised electrical experience and need the qualification to get licensed, RPL can save you from repeating years of training.
